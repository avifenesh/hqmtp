# Peer Review

## Reviewer: charlie

## Summary

Paper tests compression schemes on co-trained multi-token-prediction draft heads in Qwen/DeepSeek models. Authors compare vocabulary trimming, quantization (8-bit, NVFP4), channel pruning, low-rank factorization, and distillation across three evaluation corpora of increasing domain shift. Main claim: compression families split into "fidelity cuts" (uniform quantization, max 1.9 retention-point tax on shifted data) versus "function cuts" (structural pruning/low-rank/distillation, 10–19 retention-point tax). Recipe trims vocabulary to 32k hottest tokens, quantizes to NVFP4, delivers 1.8–2.7× speedup at 212–259MB with distribution-exact outputs. Audit runs compressed head beside reference in-distribution, uses disagreement rate and harm-per-disagreement to classify compression family and predict off-distribution damage (Spearman 1.0 on hardest suite).

## Major concerns

### 1. Headline speedup claims rest on underspecified aggregation that hides single-prompt variance the paper itself flags as blocking

**Where**: §4.0, Table 1, Abstract headline "1.8 to 2.7×".

**Why it matters**: Paper states single-prompt sampled cells swing **12 acceptance points** across seeds, twenty-seed measurement on one prompt spans **57 points (sd=13)**, and suite-level aggregation with three seeds brings spread "under 1.5 points." Every speedup in Table 1 aggregates 80 or 20 prompts with **exactly three seeds**. The protocol the authors claim "makes sampled-decoding comparisons reproducible" is tested at 20 seeds on one prompt, then applied at three seeds across suites, with no evidence that three-seed suite aggregation stabilizes the *speedup* metric (only acceptance is shown to stabilize). If a single prompt's acceptance swings 57 points over twenty seeds (with sd=13 format-independent), and acceptance directly determines speedup, then speedup inherits that instability. The paper provides zero empirical demonstration that three seeds suffice to stabilize end-to-end tok/s measurements, only that acceptance retention on teacher-forced positions (a different measurement setting) stabilizes. The Abstract's "1.8 to 2.7×" is therefore an aggregate over unknown per-prompt variance, reported at a seed count (3) that is 7× below the count (20) needed to measure the variance on a single prompt.

**What would address it**: Report per-prompt tok/s standard deviation across the three seeds for every aggregate cell in Table 1. If per-prompt sd is comparable to the inter-arm gaps the paper uses to rank methods (e.g., 9B-long greedy: trim-Q8 292.3 vs trim-NVFP4 282.5, a 10 tok/s gap), the ranking claims collapse. Alternatively, re-run the key cells (9B-long, 27B-long) at 20 seeds and show that tok/s variance drops below the inter-arm gaps at the reported aggregation level. Without this, the headline speedup numbers and their rankings are not reproducible even under the paper's own protocol.

### 2. The audit's claim of "Spearman 1.0" off-distribution ranking rests on seven data points with identical relative ordering, a result whose probability under any reasonable null the paper does not compute

**Where**: §5.3, "Spearman 1.0, exact p = 0.0002."

**Why it matters**: Spearman 1.0 means the seven arms' in-distribution disagreement rates rank in exactly the same order as their off-distribution taxes. The reported p-value (0.0002) is the two-tailed asymptotic test against random permutations, which for n=7 has only 7! = 5040 possible rank orderings. Exact permutation p for perfect rank agreement is 1/5040 ≈ 0.0002, so the test is reporting "this ordering is unlikely if all orderings were equally probable." But the null "all orderings equally probable" is **not the scientific null** here. The scientific null is "in-distribution disagreement does not predict off-distribution damage," which allows many orderings where high-disagreement arms also have high damage (e.g., any monotonic relationship, not just the exact observed one). Under a weaker but more realistic null—any of the (7 choose 2) = 21 pairwise rank-order flips that preserve broad monotonicity—the p-value inflates substantially. More critically, with only seven compression schemes, three of which are quantization variants (reference, Q8, NVFP4) expected to rank adjacently by design, and two of which are low-rank variants also expected to cluster, the effective degrees of freedom are far below seven. The paper provides no power analysis, no assessment of whether n=7 arms suffice to distinguish the audit signal from noise, and no replication on an independent compression set that wasn't designed by the same authors who selected these seven.

**What would address it**: (1) Report exact permutation p-value under a scientifically grounded null that allows for general monotonic association, not just random shuffling (e.g., Kendall's tau or a regression-based test). (2) Compute statistical power: given n=7 and the observed effect sizes, what is the probability of detecting Spearman < 0.8 if the true correlation were 0.8? If power is below 0.5, the test cannot distinguish "perfect" from "strong but imperfect." (3) Validate the audit on an independent compression suite (e.g., different pruning ratios, different distillation targets, gradient-based pruning) not selected by the authors, ideally chosen adversarially. (4) Acknowledge that the 27B replication uses the **same seven arm types** on the same model family; replication of the correlation on seven identically-chosen schemes is not independent evidence.

### 3. The "function-cut" mechanism (selection on finite samples misses rare-firing parameters) predicts damage should concentrate in rare-content bins, but low-rank arms damage all bins uniformly—contradicting the proposed explanation

**Where**: §4.2 ("low-rank arms... damage all bins"), §6.1 ("rare function, rare evidence"), §6.3 ("rarity-concentration as the audit signal... failed").

**Why it matters**: The paper's mechanistic story is that calibration-based selection (pruning, low-rank on calibration data, distillation) cannot see rare-firing function because rare content provides negligible mass over finite samples. This predicts damage should localize to rare-token bins, rare languages, rare registers—the tail that was statistically invisible. Section 4.2 states pruning's tax "concentrates in rare-token bins" (supporting the mechanism), but then immediately states low-rank arms "damage all bins," and §6.3 explicitly reports that "rarity-concentration measures where disagreements sit... only cost separates the families." Low-rank factorization is a function cut by the paper's own taxonomy (§3.3: "chosen... by the weight matrix's own spectrum"), and SVD's large singular directions are exactly the high-mass, common-content directions—so SVD *should* preserve common content and damage rare content, per the mechanism. Instead, low-rank damages everything. This is a direct falsification of the "rare function, rare evidence" account. The paper attempts to salvage the story by saying "selective cuts choose different function to delete, and shift surfaces the deletion either way," which is a retreat to "function cuts cause damage" without explaining *why* or *how* the selection fails. If SVD (data-free, spectrum-based) damages all bins and calibrated SVD (conditioned on observed content) also damages all bins, the unifying explanation cannot be "finite calibration samples miss rare content"—it must be something else, and the paper does not identify it.

**What would address it**: Provide a unified mechanistic explanation that accounts for both pruning (damage localizes to rare bins) and low-rank (damage uniform across bins). The current story works for pruning and fails for SVD. One candidate: dimensionality reduction itself (pruning's channel budget, SVD's rank budget) forces the head's representation into a subspace that cannot simultaneously cover the full content range the original head served, and which part of the range gets sacrificed depends on the selection statistic—but the damage rate is determined by the dimensionality cut, independent of the statistic. Test this by comparing data-free pruning (e.g., magnitude-based, no calibration) against calibrated pruning at equal keep-ratios: if both pay similar taxes and differ only in *where* the damage lands, dimensionality is the cause and the "finite sample" story is a special case, not the root. If data-free pruning is cheaper, the paper's calibration-centric story is wrong. Alternatively, abandon the mechanistic claim and present the result as empirical: "function cuts cost more under shift; we tested six selection schemes and all failed; we do not know why SVD damages uniformly."

### 4. The "trim is shift-proof" claim rests on asserting that hot-vocabulary coverage is the ceiling, but the paper never tests whether trim damages *acceptance conditional on coverage*—i.e., whether the draft's ranking quality degrades on the remaining 95–99% of tokens

**Where**: §3.1, §4.2 ("trim's ceiling is hot coverage: 95 to 99%... This is the structural reason trim carries no distribution-shift tax"), Table 1 (trim alone costs 0–3 acceptance points at 27B-long, 0.2 points).

**Why it matters**: The argument is: trim deletes 87% of vocabulary rows, keeps the 32k hottest, drafts can only propose hot tokens, so trim's accuracy ceiling is hot coverage (the fraction of generated tokens that are hot). The implicit claim is that trim does no damage *within its coverage*—that the 95–99% of tokens the trimmed head can still propose are drafted as accurately as before. But the paper never isolates this. The 0.2 acceptance-point cost at 27B-long could be (a) purely from the 1–5% of tokens now out-of-vocabulary, or (b) from degraded ranking quality on in-vocabulary tokens due to removing 87% of the output projection's rows and thereby changing the head's loss landscape, training dynamics, or logit geometry. The recipe pairs trim with quantization, so every reported trim result is actually trim+verification, and verification is full-vocabulary, which means the *target* can still emit cold tokens—the draft just cannot propose them. If cold tokens appear in high-probability positions (which they do: special tokens, names, technical terms), the trimmed draft loses those positions even though the target would have verified them, and this is not a "coverage ceiling"—it is a ranking failure. The paper does not report acceptance separately on hot-only positions versus all positions, does not report how often cold tokens appear in top-k positions of the reference head's output, and does not measure whether the draft's ranking *within the hot vocabulary* degrades (e.g., by comparing the trimmed head's top-1 hot-token accuracy against the reference head's top-1 hot-token accuracy, both restricted to hot vocabulary).

**What would address it**: (1) Measure the reference head's acceptance when *it* is also restricted to proposing only hot tokens (i.e., simulate trim without removing rows, just mask the output). If this matches the trimmed head's acceptance, trim truly does no damage beyond coverage. If it is higher, trim damages ranking quality. (2) Report the fraction of positions where the reference head's top-1 prediction is a cold token, broken down by corpus (in-distribution, same-register, domain-shift). If this fraction is non-negligible and grows with shift, trim's "shift-proof" claim is false. (3) Provide per-token attribution: of the 0.2 acceptance points trim costs at 27B-long, how many come from positions where the reference would have proposed a cold token (unavoidable loss) versus positions where both heads can only propose hot tokens but the trimmed head picks the wrong one (avoidable ranking damage)?

### 5. Table 1's ranking conflicts internally and the text's interpretation of the conflicts is post-hoc: trim-Q8 beats trim-NVFP4 on 9B-long greedy (the cell the paper uses to justify the "block codec owns acceptance" story), but NVFP4 beats Q8 on all other cells—no unified mechanism is offered

**Where**: Table 1, §4.1 ("The 4-bit cost sits in the block, and a mixed export recovers it"), Abstract ("within the family, the block codec picks the winner").

**Why it matters**: The evidence for "block codec sets the remaining acceptance" is that mixing NVFP4 projection + Q4_0 block at byte-identical 212MB yields 58.3% acceptance versus all-NVFP4's 54.7% on 9B-long, and "takes that suite outright" at 2.09×. But all-NVFP4 already beat Q8 on three of the four aggregate cells in Table 1 (9B-short greedy 262.8 vs 256.5, 27B-short both flavors, 27B-long greedy 127.5 vs 123.6). The one cell where Q8 wins (9B-long greedy, 292.3 vs 282.5) is the cell the authors use to justify testing the mixed arm, but that mixed arm (2.09×) is only reported as a multiplier, not as absolute tok/s, so we cannot verify it actually beats Q8's 292.3. If the mixed arm's 2.09× on 9B-long greedy corresponds to (2.09 × 143.0) = 299 tok/s, it beats Q8's 292.3 by 7 tok/s—well within the single-prompt variance the paper flags as 12 acceptance points, which at 54.7% acceptance and K=4 could easily correspond to ±10 tok/s or more. The paper does not report the mixed arm's performance on any other cell, does not explain why Q4_0 block beats NVFP4 block on 9B-long but NVFP4 beats Q8 everywhere else, and does not provide a mechanistic story for why block codec choice should be suite-dependent (short vs. long prompt). The "block codec owns acceptance" conclusion is drawn from one mixed-arm result on one suite with no error bars, no replication, and no mechanistic account of why the codec interaction is suite-specific.

**What would address it**: (1) Report the mixed arm's absolute tok/s (not just multiplier) and acceptance on all eight cells of Table 1, with per-prompt sd across the three seeds. (2) If the mixed arm's advantage over NVFP4 is significant and replicable, explain why NVFP4 projection + Q4_0 block outperforms all-NVFP4, and why this advantage appears on long prompts but not short prompts. (3) If the advantage is within noise, retract the "block codec picks the winner" claim and acknowledge that all-NVFP4 is the recipe. (4) Reconcile why Q8 beats NVFP4 on 9B-long greedy but loses on all other cells: is this statistical noise (expected under the 1.5-point suite-aggregation noise bound), or a real prompt-length or model-size interaction? If the latter, the recipe cannot be "trim + NVFP4" without a prompt-length or model-size conditional.

### 6. The six-scheme selection study (§4.4) is reported as "all failed identically" but the text provides no quantitative results—no per-scheme retention numbers, no comparison of failure modes, no evidence that "identically" means anything beyond "all paid a tax"

**Where**: §4.4, §6.1 ("We measured it... 12 to 100 documents, and an online mask over the live stream itself").

**Why it matters**: The paper's strongest claim about the mechanism's generality is that six different selection schemes all fail to escape the function-cut tax. This is the evidence that the tax is intrinsic to selection from finite samples, not a quirk of one statistic. But §4.4 provides **zero quantitative results**. We are told "six selection schemes fail identically," shown one retention number from §4.2 (pruning at 25% keep costs 16.8 points, at 50% keep costs 10.2 points), and told that divergence-conditioned selection "restores less accuracy than... plain global mass." We are not told: (1) what retention each of the six schemes achieves on the domain-shift suite, (2) how "identically" is quantified (do they all land in a 2-point band? 5-point?), (3) whether the six schemes differ in *where* their damage lands (rare bins vs. all bins) even if the total damage is similar, or (4) how much calibration data each scheme used (the text says "12 to 100 documents" but does not map this to schemes). If Wanda-style activation×weight and vocabulary-conditioned hot-logit selection both cost 16 points but one damages rare bins and the other damages all bins, they did not "fail identically"—they failed for different reasons, and the mechanism is not unified. If the six schemes span a 10-point range (say, 10–20 retention points), "identically" is a mischaracterization.

**What would address it**: Add a table: six rows (global mass, Wanda-style, wide calibration, online EMA, hot-logit, divergence-conditioned), three columns (retention on domain-shift suite, rare-bin damage concentration metric, calibration document count), at one fixed keep-ratio (say, 25%). Show the retention numbers cluster within 2–3 points and the rare-bin concentration varies (or does not), and then the "identically" claim is substantiated. Without this, the six-scheme study is an assertion, not a result.

## Minor concerns

- §2.2: "The verifier measures it directly" overstates the claim. The verifier measures whether compressed head ≠ reference head at top-1. It does not measure whether the compressed head's function *for the task of speculative decoding* degraded, because verification itself compensates for drafting errors. A draft error only "costs one round of wasted speculation" if the verify pass is cheap relative to drafting; if the target is large and the draft is tiny, wasting a verify pass is expensive, and the "error-tolerant by construction" framing undersells the cost.

- §3.0: "1.33GB per drafted token" is unclear whether this is FP16 or INT8. The text later says "1.06GB of 8-bit weights read per drafted token" (projection) + "0.26GB" (block), implying the 1.33GB is 8-bit. But the reference head in Table 1 is presumably FP16 or BF16 (standard HuggingFace checkpoint precision), which would be 2.66GB. Clarify.

- §4.0: "Each cell reports acceptance... for greedy decoding and for temperature 0.7 with three sampling seeds." If greedy cells also use three seeds, what varies across seeds in a deterministic decode? If greedy uses one seed and sampled uses three, this should be stated. If greedy cells are three-seed averages of identical runs (and therefore just one measurement repeated three times), reporting them as aggregates is misleading.

- §4.2: "The family gap of 14.5 retention points is significant at the permutation-test floor for seven arms (p = 0.029)." What is the "permutation-test floor"? If this means the smallest p-value achievable with 7! permutations is 1/5040 ≈ 0.0002, then p = 0.029 is not "at the floor," it is 145× above it. If this is a t-test or Mann-Whitney test on two groups (fidelity cuts vs. function cuts), state it. If it is a permutation test on the family labels, describe the test statistic. The current phrasing is opaque.

- §4.2: "every function-cut tax individually excludes zero at 95% confidence." What is the confidence interval computed over? If each arm is one aggregate cell (80 or 20 prompts, three seeds), and the retention percentage is a single number per arm, there is no within-arm variance to construct a CI unless you are bootstrapping over prompts or seeds. Describe the CI method.

- §5.3: "The 27B replication reproduces the exact ranking over its own seven arms, so the instrument transfers across model scale unchanged." This is not independent replication; it is the same seven compression schemes applied to a second model in the same family. True transfer would require testing the audit on a different model family (e.g., Llama, Mistral) or a different architecture (e.g., MoE). Rephrase as "reproduced on a second model scale within the same family."

- Table 1 caption should state the plain-decode baseline tok/s denominators (currently, readers must divide to extract them), and should clarify whether the reference head is FP16, BF16, or INT8.

- §6.2: "features stored in superposition" is asserted without citation or prior establishment that the head's output projection is in a superposition regime. If this is the authors' interpretation, state it as such. If it is drawing on superposition literature (e.g., Elhage et al.), cite it.

- §7: "FR-Spec trims the draft vocabulary at load time and reports 1 to 3% end-to-end gain in llama.cpp." No arXiv ID is provided for FR-Spec (only llama.cpp is named). If this is an implementation detail in a GitHub repo rather than a published method, clarify.

- §8.1 playbook row 2: "Stop. Pruning and low-rank buy at most 90MB more" — 90MB is not derived in the paper. The 9B head is 1.33GB; trim brings it to ~400MB (32k vocab × 4096 dim × 8-bit ≈ 134MB projection + 0.26GB block ≈ 400MB); quantizing to NVFP4 brings it to 212MB. Pruning at 50% keep would halve the block (130MB saved), not 90MB. Either correct the number or show the arithmetic.

- §8.2: "Other architectures' heads may sit differently on the cost curve." This undersells the generality concern. The taxonomy's empirical evidence covers exactly two models (9B, 27B) in one family (Qwen3.5/3.6), plus one "independently built third configuration" in the same engine. The mechanistic claim (§6) has no architecture-specific ingredient, but the *taxonomy's boundaries* (1.9 points for fidelity, 10–19 for function) are presented as general constants when they are measured on two data points. Rewrite to acknowledge the taxonomy's qualitative claim (fidelity vs. function) may generalize but the quantitative thresholds are Qwen-specific until tested elsewhere.

## Verdict

**Major revision**

Paper presents useful practitioner contribution (zero-training compression recipe, pre-deployment audit) and identifies real qualitative phenomenon (uniform-precision cuts robust under shift, structural cuts not). But core quantitative claims rest on underspecified statistics that the paper's own variance measurements call into question (speedup rankings from three-seed aggregates when single-prompt single-metric swings 57 points over 20 seeds), on small-n hypothesis tests that cannot distinguish perfect from strong correlation (Spearman 1.0 on seven arms with clustered compression types, no power analysis), and on a mechanistic explanation that fails for half the function-cut family (low-rank damages uniformly, contradicting the "rare content = rare evidence" story). The six-scheme selection study that would make the mechanism claim compelling is reported as "all failed identically" with zero per-scheme numbers. The audit instrument is promising but validated on the same seven compression schemes the authors designed, offering no evidence it generalizes to adversarially-chosen or gradient-based cuts outside the zero-training envelope. Revision should: (1) report per-prompt tok/s variance and justify that three seeds suffice to stabilize rankings, (2) provide power analysis and independent validation for the audit (different compression suite, adversarial selection), (3) either unify the mechanism to explain both pruning and low-rank damage patterns or retreat to an empirical taxonomy without mechanistic claims, (4) add quantitative results table for the six-scheme study, and (5) measure trim's damage conditional on coverage (ranking quality on hot-only tokens). With these, the recipe and audit are solid contributions; without them, the headline claims are not reproducible under the paper's own protocol and the mechanism is contradicted by the paper's own evidence.
